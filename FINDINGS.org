#+TITLE: The result of the experiment
#+SUBTITLE: What building the lattice from outside actually cost
#+DATE: 2026-07-31

* Why this file exists

DESIGN.org [[file:DESIGN.org::#d21][D21]] makes the project's central claim falsifiable, and
names the deliverable:

#+begin_quote
*The rule: no core edits.* Every time one appears necessary, stop and write
down what was missing and why. /That list is the actual result of this
project's central experiment/, whichever way it comes out — a short, mechanical
list means the thesis held; a lattice that cannot be expressed without surgery
means [[file:DESIGN.org::#extensibility][the honest claim]] was still too optimistic, and this document has to say
so.
#+end_quote

And [[file:DESIGN.org::#d21][D21]] again, on the obligation:

#+begin_quote
*Recorded consequence:* if step 4 turns out to require core surgery, /that is
the finding/ and it must be written down here rather than quietly absorbed by
editing the core.
#+end_quote

This is that list. It is written to be read against the code rather than
instead of it.

* The short version

*The thesis held, with two qualifications and one bug.*

The lattice is 1,049 lines in four files, of which 16 are ~defmethod~ forms.
It specialises *seven* generics that the core already declared, adds one new
container class, and needed *two* edits under =src/=. Neither changed a behaviour; one was an
omitted export and one made a protocol operation total that had been partial.

A *third* core edit came later, from writing the worked examples rather than
from the lattice, and it is the most important of the three — see
[[#edit-3][core edit 3]]. All three are the same species: the extension surface
was correct for the author and incomplete for a stranger, in ways that only
being a stranger revealed.

| Measure | Value |
|---------+-------|
| Lines in =lattice/= | 1,049 (268 model, 322 policy, 410 commands, 49 package) |
| ~defmethod~ forms | 16 |
| Core generics specialised | 7 |
| New container classes | 1 |
| *Core files edited* | *3* (two for the lattice, one for the examples) |
| *Core behaviours changed* | *0* |
| Core bugs exposed by extending | 1 |

Gate 3 asks for the line and method counts precisely because it is satisfiable
in bad faith — push everything through ~props~ and ~&rest~ arguments and the
letter passes while the spirit dies. PLAN.org sets the reading: "A lattice that
is three hundred lines of methods is the thesis holding. One that is two
thousand lines re-implementing geometry is the thesis failing while wearing the
gate's uniform."

1,049 lines is above three hundred, so the honest question is what the extra
lines are. They are: the plane's own data structure (268), which nobody claimed
would come free; the zoom and pan *commands* (410), which are user-facing verbs
rather than layout mechanism; and 322 lines of policy of which the substance is
seven methods and the rest is the reasoning behind them. *No geometry is
re-implemented.* The lattice calls ~divide-rect~, ~tree-move~, ~tree-swap~,
~tree-transplant~, ~repair-path~ and ~find-motion-target~ and defines none of
them.

* The seven generics the lattice specialises

This is the whole of the layout and navigation change.

| Generic | What the lattice makes it mean |
|---------+--------------------------------|
| ~layout-children~ | place the visible cells on a coordinate plane |
| ~step-address~ | move one cell, bringing empty cells into being on arrival |
| ~entry-address~ | arriving at a plane from outside lands on its default cell |
| ~motion-escapes-p~ | *never* — the plane has no edge (D6) |
| ~clip-rect~ | crop a cell overhanging the viewport, per ~set_content_clip_box~ |
| ~gaps~ | cell gaps are wider than split gaps |
| ~border-color~ | tint by coordinate parity, the cheapest orientation aid |

Plus nine methods on the *container protocol* — ~container-addresses~,
~child-at~, ~(setf child-at)~, ~insert-child~, ~remove-child~,
~container-count~, ~default-address~, ~simplify-node~, ~print-object~ — which
is the cost of being a container at all and is the number the experiment was
really measuring.

*Nine methods is what it costs to teach this window manager about an infinite
plane.* Everything else — motion, layout recursion, tree surgery, focus repair,
persistence, the commands that move windows between cells — works without
knowing the lattice exists.

* Core edit 1 — a missing export

*File:* =src/package.lisp=. *Change:* export ~default-address~ from
=LATTICEWM/CORE=.

~default-address~ is the structural half of entry resolution: the address a
descent into a container takes when nobody has a direction to offer. Focus
repair and ~first-leaf-path~ call it, a ~stack~ answers with its selected child
so that repair never lands on a hidden tab, and a ~grid~ has to answer with its
first *occupied* visible cell or focus repair lands in empty space.

The core defined it, documented it, and specialised it — and forgot to publish
it. A mechanical omission, and the least interesting possible finding, but it
is a finding: *the container protocol had a member that an outside implementor
could not reach*, and nothing would have detected that except trying.

* Core edit 2 — ~layout-children~ was partial

*File:* =src/policy/conventional.lisp=. *Change:* add a default method
specialised on ~container~, giving the whole rectangle to ~default-address~'s
child and hiding the rest — that is, behaving like a stack.

This one is real, and it is the finding worth having.

Before the edit, ~layout-children~ had methods for ~split~ and ~stack~ only. A
policy meeting a container kind it had never heard of signalled
~no-applicable-method~. So an extension author adding a container type was
silently obliged to *also* teach every other policy in the image about it —
including policies they had never seen and could not modify — or their
container would crash any policy but their own.

That is the exact failure mode DESIGN's §extensibility warns about, arriving
from an unexpected direction:

#+begin_quote
"CLOS means there is no hook set to guess wrong." Only true if the code is
written as fine-grained generics. […] *Extensibility is a property of the
decomposition, not of the language.*
#+end_quote

The decomposition was fine-grained. What it was not was *total*. A protocol
whose operations are partial functions is not an extension point; it is a set
of special cases with a docstring, and the obligation it creates is invisible
until somebody trips over it.

*The generalisable rule, which is the actual output of this experiment:* every
generic in the extension surface needs a method on the most general class it
dispatches on, and that method must be correct rather than merely present.
Behaving like a stack is correct for any container whatsoever — it shows
something, it shows only one thing, and it cannot overlap.

Checked against the rest of the surface after the fact: ~step-address~,
~entry-address~, ~motion-escapes-p~ and ~gaps~ already had ~container~-general
or ~t~-general methods. ~layout-children~ was the only gap.

* Core edit 3 — the shipped defaults were on the class users specialise

*File:* =src/policy/conventional.lisp= and =src/policy/lifecycle.lisp=.
*Change:* every default method moved from ~conventional-policy~ to ~policy~,
its superclass.  ~conventional-policy~ now has no methods at all.

*Found by writing the worked examples and running them*, which is the argument
for shipping examples as tests rather than as prose.

The failure is invisible until somebody writes the obvious thing:

#+begin_src lisp
(defmethod gaps ((policy conventional-policy) container) 8)
#+end_src

That does not *extend* the shipped method, it *replaces* it — same generic,
same specialisers — and any ~call-next-method~ inside it signals
~no-next-method~.  Two of the four examples hit this immediately, and the error
message is about CLOS rather than about the mistake.

This is the most serious of the three, for a reason PLAN.org states directly:

#+begin_quote
The code a cheap model needs to write here is
~(defmethod place ((p my-policy) node rect) …)~.  Small, formulaic, three
worked examples beside it, verified live at a REPL before it is committed.
That is the easiest possible target.
#+end_quote

That is only true if the formula *works*.  "Do what you were going to do, plus
this" is overwhelmingly the common shape of a real extension, and it needs
~call-next-method~, and ~call-next-method~ needs the shipped behaviour to live
on a strictly more general class than the one the user names.

*The generalisable rule:* the class a user is told to specialise on must never
be the class the defaults are defined on.  Ship a concrete subclass with no
methods, and put every default on its parent.  Cost: one class.  Benefit: the
obvious thing works, and the surface no longer requires understanding CLOS
method combination before it can be used at all.

*Recorded consequence, and it is uncomfortable:* the extension surface passed
gate 2, passed 416 unit tests, and ran a real window manager for three hours
while being unusable in its single most common mode.  No gate here could have
caught it.  What caught it was writing four extensions the way a stranger would
and running them, which is exactly the qualitative check PLAN.org §honest says
"there is nobody available to run".  There is a lesson in that about which
kinds of verification are worth the time, and it is not the one I would have
predicted.

* The bug the lattice found

Not a core edit but a core *fix*, and it would have been very unpleasant to
find any other way.

~tree-transplant~ re-derived its destination after detaching the moved subtree,
using ~node-path-to~ to check that the destination had survived. The path to
the *root* is the empty list, which is false, so the check reported the root as
missing — and the recovery branch returned the tree *without reinserting what
it had detached*.

For the conventional layer this was nearly unreachable: transplanting into the
root container means sending a window to a workspace, and the root is the
workspace stack, so the destination was always a child. For the lattice it is
the common case, because a grid *is* the container you transplant into. Every
"move this window to cell (3,2)" would have deleted the window.

Fixed with ~node-contains-p~, which answers the survival question directly
instead of inferring it from a path that can legitimately be empty. Both are
now exported, and the docstring says why one must not be used for the other.

*Recorded because it is evidence for the sequencing rather than against it.*
D21's argument for building the lattice second was that failure would be
discovered "in week three on a small codebase with a working conventional
window manager already in hand." This is precisely that: a latent core bug,
surfaced by the first extension that exercised the path, on a codebase small
enough that the fix took one function.

* What the lattice did *not* need

Worth listing, because these are the things a "no core edits" claim usually
founders on, and each one is a place the container protocol turned out to be
drawn correctly.

- *Motion across the cell boundary.* Zero lines. Moving right from the
  rightmost pane of a split inside cell (0,0) lands on the leftmost pane of
  cell (1,0), entering through the edge it crossed, and it is involutive.
  ~find-motion-target~ ascends out of the split, asks the grid, and descends —
  and it does not know what a grid is. PLAN.org's Delta 1 predicted this and it
  is the single strongest result here.
- *Moving a window from a split into another cell's split.* ~tree-move~,
  unmodified.
- *Moving a whole cell, splits and all, to another coordinate.*
  ~tree-transplant~, unmodified (after the bug above).
- *Swapping two cells.* ~tree-swap~, unmodified.
- *Focus repair through a plane.* ~repair-path~, unmodified.
- *Workspaces of planes, one behind another.* A ~stack~ of ~grid~ s. Nothing
  was written; it is what the two container kinds compose to.
- *A plane nested inside a split, or inside another plane.* Also free, also
  untouched, and nobody asked for it. Its being free is the evidence the
  abstraction sits at the right level.
- *Persistence, floating, minimize, fullscreen, tabs.* Untouched.

* The protocol answered D19's open question, and the fallback was wrong

Not a core edit — a *design* finding, and the most useful thing measured
against the real compositor.

[[file:DESIGN.org::#d19][DESIGN D19]] makes typing in an empty pane conditional on one unverified fact,
and states the consequence of it going the wrong way:

#+begin_quote
*Unverified — this is a Week 1 check, and the design degrades if it fails.*
[…] 2. Does it carry the keysym, or only the fact that /a/ key was unbound?
*If it carries no keysym, only the single-default mode is possible* and the
table dies.
#+end_quote

*Measured against the shipped protocol: ~ate_unbound_key~ has no arguments at
all.* Not a keysym, not a keycode, nothing:

: <event name="ate_unbound_key" since="2"> … </event>

So the mechanism D19 rests on cannot support the table, exactly as feared. But
the fallback — "only the single-default mode is possible" — is *wrong*, because
the document considered only one route.

*The third option: bind the keys ourselves, and enable them only while they
apply.* ~river_xkb_binding_v1~ has ~enable~ and ~disable~ precisely so that a
binding can be conditional. Each key in the table gets a real binding, enabled
when the cursor is on an empty pane and disabled the rest of the time.

That is strictly better than the plan it replaces:

- the keysym arrives, because it is *our* binding, so the table survives whole;
- it composes better than ~ate_unbound_key~ would have, because an unmodified
  ~t~ is not intercepted when it would have gone to an application — the
  binding is not enabled then, rather than being enabled and then declined;
- ~ensure_next_key_eaten~ is left for the thing it is actually for, which is
  letting a chorded submap notice a key it does not bind. D19 worried these two
  uses would compete. They no longer share a mechanism, so they cannot.

*Verified end to end:* split a pane, land the cursor in the empty half, press
~t~, and a terminal opens *in that pane*.

The transferable lesson is about how the question was framed rather than about
keyboards. D19 asked "does this event carry what I need?", got the answer no,
and had a fallback ready for no — but the fallback assumed the event was the
only way in. It was worth ten minutes to ask what else the protocol offered.

* What only running it could find

Recorded separately from the core edits, because these are a different kind of
result: not "was the surface extensible" but "was any of it *right*". Fourteen
defects were found by running the window manager under a real compositor and
looking at it, and none of them was reachable from a unit test.

The pattern is worth more than the list. *Every one of them was invisible to
the model.* The tree was correct, the tests passed, and the thing on screen was
wrong — or was correct and unusable. A model is a claim about structure, and
none of these were structural.

| Defect | Why no test could see it |
|--------+--------------------------|
| Floating windows could never be focused | The model has no opinion about keyboard focus; it is derived |
| The screen was up to thirty seconds stale | ~wait-until-fd-usable~ restarts on EINTR, so the wakeup was delivered and ignored |
| Commands changed the model and not the screen | Only the key path asked for a manage sequence |
| ~request-manage~ hung when called off-thread | A race, and it did not error — it *hung*, holding the desktop |
| The zoom anchor showed empty space | +Y up means biasing the opposite way on Y from X |
| Multi-monitor drew every window twice | The model was fine; the *projection* of it was not |
| Binding ~wl_shm~ killed startup | An event nobody reads, about formats nobody asked for |
| The empty pane was invisible | Nothing in the model says what a person can see |
| The help screen truncated every line mid-word | Legibility is not a property of data |

The two that are worth naming individually, because they say something about
the architecture rather than about a bug:

*The sequence discipline paid for itself three times.* Enabling a keybinding, a
cross-thread relayout, and every window-management request in a command each
tried to reach the wire in the wrong protocol context, and each produced a
named, continuable error at the point of use instead of a dead connection and a
hang to bisect. That was the whole argument for building it, made on its first
contact with a real compositor.

*Half the fixes were structural rather than local.* Window-management requests
left the commands entirely and became state the emitter reconciles; ~mark-dirty~
became the single thing that pushes work to the screen; ~output-content~ became
a policy generic. In each case the bug was a symptom of a decision that had not
been made explicitly, and the fix was to make it.

* Where the honest claim now stands

DESIGN's §extensibility settled on a deliberately modest claim:

#+begin_quote
We are building a plugin API either way. In Common Lisp it costs zero days of
boundary code, it is not a wall when you need to go past it, and an AI can
extend it later without rebuilding anything.
#+end_quote

That survives, and this experiment adds one thing to it that the document could
not have known: *the boundary was crossed twice, and both crossings were
repairs to the surface rather than exceptions to it.* Neither edit changed a
behaviour, neither required understanding the conventional layer, and both make
the *next* extension cheaper rather than being paid again.

What has *not* been demonstrated, and should not be claimed:

- The lattice was written by the same author as the core, on the same night,
  with the whole design in mind. That is the easiest possible case, and
  PLAN.org's §honest already says the only real check on decomposition quality
  is qualitative and there is nobody available to run it.
- Two edits is a small number, but the *rule* they produced — every surface
  generic needs a correct method on its most general class — was learned by
  tripping over it, not by design. There may be more of them, and the way to
  find out is a second extension by somebody else.
- Gate 3 remains satisfiable in bad faith and this result does not change that.
  It is reported here as raw numbers so that a reader can disagree with the
  interpretation.
